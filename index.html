01 · Squares, Caravaggio & Roman baking · Allow 3–4 hoursThe Pantheon, Caravaggio & a forno pause

From Trevi to the Pantheon, then a pause with Caravaggio before Piazza Navona and the bakeries around Campo de’ Fiori. I’d leave time for the church rather than hurry from square to square.

Your route. Trevi → Pantheon → San Luigi dei Francesi → Piazza Navona → Campo de’ Fiori → Via del Pellegrino.

Before you set out

Time to enjoy it. 3–4 hours with Caravaggio and one food stop; longer for the Pantheon interior.
Underfoot. Mostly level streets, with cobbles and church entrance steps.
My food pause. Pizza bianca or rossa at Forno Campo de’ Fiori; for a full Roman meal, reserve Cesare al Pellegrino. Choose the day’s seasonal cooking alongside the familiar pasta dishes.

Pause over the contrast of light and shadow; this is one of the art stops I would make time for on this walk.

Two pizza traditions
Roman or Neapolitan?

Traditional round Roman pizza is thin and crisp, with a low edge — the texture locals call scrocchiarella. Neapolitan pizza is softer and more flexible, with a raised, airy rim called the cornicione. The Roman pastry tradition
Three addresses to remember

A sweeter side of Rome

Leave room for a maritozzo in the morning, a slice of ricotta and sour-cherry tart on a walk, or a small parcel of traditional sweets to take home. I have kept this selection deliberately short: three established workshops, each with a distinct place in Rome’s pastry culture.

What to taste in Rome

Maritozzo, ricotta e visciole & the older Roman repertoire

Maritozzo con la panna

A soft, lightly sweet leavened bun, split open and generously filled with fresh whipped cream. Its pleasure lies in the contrast between airy bread and cool cream. Order “un maritozzo con la panna” and enjoy it freshly filled. The older quaresimale version, associated with Lent, belongs to a different, fruit-studded tradition.

Crostata di ricotta e visciole

A pastry tart pairing creamy ricotta with the bright acidity of sour cherries. A defining sweet of Rome’s Jewish baking tradition; the contrast of fruit, cheese and pastry is what makes it memorable.

Pizza di Beridde

Also called pizza ebraica: a dense sweet bake with nuts, raisins and candied fruit. Despite its name, it has little in common with savoury pizza. A small piece makes a satisfying accompaniment to coffee.

Pangiallo & panpepato

Compact sweets built around dried fruit, nuts and, in varying recipes, spices. They belong especially to the festive repertoire. Ask the workshop what is available; recipes and seasonal production vary.

Bignè di San Giuseppe

Cream-filled choux pastries, traditionally associated in Rome with St Joseph’s Day on 19 March. The fried version is a particular Roman favourite; baked versions are also found. Look for them in season rather than expecting every counter to carry them year-round.

Paying & tipping
Paying in Rome

Tipping in Italy is optional; a modest extra amount for excellent service is appreciated. A restaurant’s coperto is a cover charge, not a tip. Cards are widely accepted. If a card terminal or ATM asks whether to charge you in your home currency or EUR, choosing EUR generally lets your own card network handle the conversion instead of the merchant’s dynamic currency conversion.

A few local habits

Rome is best explored on foot, but watch for scooters and traffic when crossing. In crowded tourist areas and on public transport, keep your phone and wallet secure as you would in any major city. Rome without surprises
A little street awareness

A few simple habits will help you explore with more peace of mind, particularly on busy public transport and around the city’s most popular sights.

On the metro, buses & in crowds

Keep your bag zipped and in front of you, and your phone and wallet out of back pockets or open bags. Be especially attentive at carriage doors, when boarding or leaving, on escalators and when a crowd gathers around an attraction.

Pickpockets may work in pairs or groups: one person distracts you while another reaches for your belongings. They can be men or women, including young women; age or appearance is no guarantee of trustworthiness. Pay attention to unnecessary crowding, repeated bumps or someone distracting you while another comes unusually close.

“Free” bracelets & small souvenirs

If someone approaches you offering a supposedly free bracelet, flower or small souvenir, do not take it or let them tie anything around your wrist. A claimed gift can turn into a demand for money. There is no need to answer personal questions, explain yourself or enter a conversation: decline briefly, or simply keep walking. If they insist, move towards a staffed shop or police officer.

The three-cup game · tre campanelle

Do not play or place a bet. This street scam involves guessing which cup hides a small ball; versions may use shells or cards. Apparent winners and encouraging spectators may be accomplices creating the impression of an easy win. Do not stop to join the crowd, show money or try to beat the game — keep walking.

Stay smart · live like a local

Good to know in my Roman home

A few practical details to help you settle in and feel at home.

Electricity
Italy uses 230 V

Italian sockets commonly use Type C, F and L plugs. A travel adapter changes the plug shape, but it does not convert voltage. Before plugging in an appliance brought from abroad, check that its voltage rating supports 230 V (for example, 100–240 V). Please never force a plug into a socket.

Power at home
One high-power appliance at a time

The apartment’s electrical supply has a limited capacity. Please avoid running several high-power appliances together — for example A/C + stove + hairdryer — as this can trip the power.

Water
Rome’s tap water is drinkable

You can drink the tap water. Around Rome you’ll also see the small public drinking fountains known as nasoni — perfect for refilling your bottle.

Hot water
The water heater has a tank
