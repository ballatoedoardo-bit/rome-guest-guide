A Roman forno is more than a bakery

In Rome, a traditional forno can be part bakery, part everyday lunch stop. Go in the morning for breads and something sweet, or come back around lunch for pizza bianca, pizza rossa, pizza alla pala and other savoury baked specialties. They’re one of my favourite ways to eat simply and locally while walking through the city.

Two pizza traditions
Roman or Neapolitan?

Traditional round Roman pizza is thin and crisp, with a low edge — the texture locals call scrocchiarella. Neapolitan pizza is softer and more flexible, with a raised, airy rim called the cornicione. Both have their own character. For a Roman-style sit-down pizza, these are my suggestions; pizza al taglio, sold in slices, is a separate everyday Roman tradition.

Paying & tipping
Paying in Rome

Tipping in Italy is optional; a modest extra amount for excellent service is appreciated. A restaurant’s coperto is a cover charge, not a tip. Cards are widely accepted. If a card terminal or ATM asks whether to charge you in your home currency or EUR, choosing EUR generally lets your own card network handle the conversion instead of the merchant’s dynamic currency conversion.

Rome without surprises
A few local habits

Rome is best explored on foot, but watch for scooters and traffic when crossing. In crowded tourist areas and on public transport, keep your phone and wallet secure as you would in any major city. Carry a refillable bottle for the nasoni. Pharmacies are marked by a green cross; for a medical emergency call 112 or 118.

Your first morning in Rome
If it were my first morning here…

I’d go out early, while Trevi is still waking up, see the fountain before the busiest part of the day, and then simply walk. From my home, the historic centre unfolds around you — Quirinale, Via del Corso, the Spanish Steps and Pantheon are all made for discovering on foot.

Use my personal Google Maps guides as you explore. Open the dedicated guide section, choose the list you need, keep your phone’s location turned on and look at the map while you walk. That way you can see which of my saved places are already around you instead of planning your day around a rigid itinerary.

And one personal tip: don’t skip Rome’s bakeries. They’re perfect for breakfast and for picking up something simple and local as you walk. For lunch, also look out for a pizzeria al taglio — the classic Roman pizza-by-the-slice shop, where you choose the pizza you want and usually pay by weight. It’s one of the easiest and most authentically Roman quick lunches. If you love viennoiserie and specialty coffee, make time for Luna near Barberini.

Stay smart · live like a local

Good to know in my Roman home

A few practical details to help you settle in and feel at home.

Electricity
Italy uses 230 V

Italian sockets commonly use Type C, F and L plugs. A travel adapter changes the plug shape, but it does not convert voltage. Before plugging in an appliance brought from abroad, check that its voltage rating supports 230 V (for example, 100–240 V). Please never force a plug into a socket.

Power at home
One high-power appliance at a time

The apartment’s electrical supply has a limited capacity. Please avoid running several high-power appliances together — for example A/C + stove + hairdryer — as this can trip the power.

Water
Rome’s tap water is drinkable

You can drink the tap water. Around Rome you’ll also see the small public drinking fountains known as nasoni — perfect for refilling your bottle.

Hot water
The water heater has a tank

Hot water is not unlimited. Please leave the middle bathroom switch ON and allow the electric water heater time to recover after several long showers.

Power outage
Too many high-power appliances running together can trip the electrical system. First switch off the high-power appliance that caused the overload, then contact me.
